# What I Didn't Learn in Business School — How Strategy Works in the Real World

**Authors:** Jay B. Barney (originator of the VRIO framework, a leading strategy academic) and Trish Gorman Clifford (practitioner/consulting background). A business novel, not a textbook — the argument is delivered through a fictional consulting engagement rather than as a list of principles, which is itself the book's thesis in structural form: strategy frameworks are clean on the page and messy the moment a real organization has to act on them.

## 1. The Setup

Justin Campbell, a freshly-minted MBA, joins a strategy consultancy and gets staffed on an engagement with **HGS Inc.**, a specialty chemicals firm sitting on a new textile technology (branded internally as "Plastiwear") with no clear plan for commercializing it. Justin's team runs the standard toolkit — market analysis, competitive-forces mapping, financial modeling — and produces a technically defensible recommendation. It goes nowhere, because the team never mapped who inside HGS actually had the authority to act on it, and who had a quiet incentive to see it fail. The novel's real subject isn't the technology or the market; it's the gap between a *correct* answer and an *implementable* one.

## 2. The Two Frameworks the Book Actually Teaches

### Five Forces (Porter)

The book is deliberately deflating about it: useful for spotting competitive threats, weak as a standalone verdict on industry attractiveness. The five forces describe competitive **winds** — the actual skill is positioning a firm relative to those winds, not avoiding them. The book's sharper point: the best-run companies sometimes turn a structurally *unfavorable* competitive position into an advantage, rather than treating an unattractive Five Forces score as a reason to walk away.

### VRIO (Barney's own framework)

The book's real spine — four sequential questions testing whether a resource or strategy is a source of **sustained** competitive advantage, not just a temporary edge:

| Question | What it's really asking |
|---|---|
| **Value** | Does this raise revenue or cut cost enough to matter? |
| **Rarity** | Do competitors already have this? |
| **Imitability** | How hard, specifically, is this to copy? |
| **Organization** | Is the firm actually structured to capture the advantage — even if Value, Rarity, and Imitability all check out? |

The **Organization** question is the one most strategy exercises skip, and it's the one HGS fails on: the technology clears Value, Rarity, and Imitability easily, but the org has no structure that can actually exploit it. The book's sharpest single line names this precisely: *"Technologies by themselves are not core competencies. But actions firms take to exploit technologies can be."* Plastiwear the chemical isn't the advantage — what HGS does organizationally around it is, and that's the part no framework can answer from the outside.

## 3. The Real Argument — Theory vs. Reality

- **Analysis gets used to confirm, not discover.** Sophisticated financial modeling is exactly as bendable to politics and bias as a gut call — it just *looks* more objective, which makes the bias harder to catch, not less present. The book states this directly: *"When managers present a point of view, assume their point of view reflects some combination of how things actually are and how they want things to be."*
- **A framework is an instrument, not a verdict.** The output is only as good as the judgment of whoever is running it. A rigorous VRIO analysis performed by someone who hasn't mapped the internal politics still produces a confidently wrong answer.
- **Map the stakeholders before the presentation, not during it.** Who can implement the recommendation, and — separately — who can quietly kill it without ever formally objecting? The room should never be the first place a key stakeholder hears the conclusion.
- **Perfect information never exists.** Every real strategic call is analysis plus intuition plus judgment under incomplete data — a framework that outputs a clean, single answer is describing the model, not the decision.

## 4. Practical Craft Lessons

The parts of the book most summaries skip, because they're about the *craft* of doing strategy work inside an organization, not the frameworks themselves:

- **Interviewing:** prepare a structured question protocol in advance, share it with interviewees before the conversation (not as a surprise), and close every interview by asking for referrals to other sources — the next person to talk to is usually more valuable than the last answer given.
- **Presenting:** address the audience, not the slides; keep energy consistent throughout instead of front-loading it; anticipate leadership's *process* questions ("how did you get here") as seriously as their *content* questions ("what should we do").
- **Career development:** *"Be well rounded but figure out where your unique area of strength is"* — generalists survive, but the people who actually matter on a team have one area of genuinely deep expertise underneath the breadth.
- **Judgment as a discipline of its own:** *"Just because you can do it, doesn't mean that you should"* — capability and correctness are different questions, and conflating them is how technically-sound recommendations end up doing real damage.

## 6. Bottom Line

VRIO and Five Forces get you a defensible answer on paper. Reading the political terrain — who benefits, who loses, who can quietly starve a good recommendation of the resources it needs — is what gets that answer actually implemented. The book's real claim isn't that the tools are wrong; it's that business school teaches the tools and calls that "strategy," when the tools are maybe half the job.

## 7. Glossary — Vocabulary Used in This Chapter

| Term / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **VRIO** | Barney's framework for testing sustained competitive advantage: Value, Rarity, Imitability, Organization. |
| **Five Forces** | Porter's framework for mapping competitive threats within an industry: rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power. |
| **Core competency** | An organizational capability — not a technology or asset by itself — that creates value, is rare, and resists imitation. |
| **Sustained competitive advantage** | An edge that persists over time, as distinct from a temporary one competitors can quickly close. |
| **Stakeholder mapping** | Identifying who has influence, interest, or authority over a decision before acting on it — as distinct from analyzing the decision's content alone. |
| **Deflating (of a framework)** | Here, presenting a well-regarded tool as more limited than its reputation suggests, on purpose. |
| **Instrument (vs. verdict)** | A tool that produces output requiring judgment to interpret, as opposed to one that hands down a final, self-justifying answer. |
