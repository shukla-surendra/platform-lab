# Silos, Politics and Turf Wars — A Leadership Fable About Destroying the Barriers That Turn Colleagues Into Competitors

**Author:** Patrick Lencioni. Like his other leadership fables, the argument is delivered through a fictional consulting narrative rather than a list of principles — the difference here is structural: instead of one company followed start to finish, the story follows one consultant across three separate client engagements, which lets the book show the same underlying fix landing in genuinely different organizational contexts rather than one lucky case.

## 1. The Setup

Jude Cousins, formerly the director of corporate communication at Hatch Technology (a financial software company), leaves to start a solo practice, Cousins Consulting. Five months in, the early excitement has curdled into anxiety: no employees, three clients, and two of those three engagements already wobbling. The book follows Jude discovering — through trial, error, and one client at a time — a single, repeatable fix for interdepartmental conflict, rather than presenting the framework fully-formed from page one.

## 2. The Core Claim — Where Silos Actually Come From

The book's central, load-bearing claim: silos are not caused by difficult people or turf-guarding personalities — they're caused by **leadership's failure to provide a compelling context for working together**. Departments don't drift into competing with each other because someone decided to be political; they drift because nobody above them ever gave the organization one shared thing to move toward, so each department quietly optimizes for its own goals by default, and those goals collide. The fix, in other words, isn't a culture initiative or a mandate to "collaborate more" — it's a specific leadership omission that has to be corrected structurally, not exhorted away.

## 3. The Framework — Three Tiers of Goals

| Tier | What it is | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| **Thematic Goal** | A single, qualitative organizational focus for a defined period — the one priority that bridges long-term vision and this period's actual work | Only one exists at a time; not numerically measured; owned jointly by the *entire* leadership team, not one department |
| **Defining Objectives** | The handful of concrete objectives that give the thematic goal actionable shape | Qualitative rather than over-quantified; time-bound; change whenever the thematic goal changes |
| **Standard Operating Objectives** | The ongoing metrics every organization already tracks — revenue, customer satisfaction, market share, quality | Real and necessary, but explicitly **not** rallying cries — the book's phrase is "critical but standard," and treating one of these as if it were the thematic goal is exactly how silos re-form |

The discipline the framework enforces: employees need to understand how their individual metric connects to the one shared thing everyone is actually working toward — and not every useful thing to track has to be a number; some objectives are better tracked as a completion date than a quantity.

## 4. The Three Vignettes

Jude applies the framework at three very different organizations, which is the book's real demonstration that the fix isn't industry-specific:

- **An upscale hotel**, where interdepartmental friction is quietly costing the guest experience.
- **Batch, a technology company** mid-merger — the vignette with the clearest business outcome: Batch not only completes the merger but goes on to use the same framework to acquire two more companies afterward.
- **A children's hospital**, which uses the framework to break down clinical/administrative silos and, over time, becomes ranked among the 20 best pediatric facilities in the country.

Cousins Consulting itself is the book's fourth, implicit case study — over eight years it grows from Jude alone to more than 15 consultants, with dedicated verticals in health care, technology, education, and nonprofits, applying the same framework to itself that it sells to clients.

## 5. Practical Lessons

- **The thematic goal must be owned by the whole leadership team, not delegated to one function.** The moment it becomes "marketing's goal" or "engineering's goal," it has already failed at the one job a thematic goal has — being shared.
- **Resist the urge to make everything measurable.** Forcing a qualitative priority into a number for the sake of a dashboard often destroys the thing that made it a real rallying point in the first place; a target date is sometimes the more honest metric than a manufactured KPI.
- **Standard operating objectives are legitimate — the failure mode is promoting one to thematic-goal status.** Revenue and customer satisfaction still matter every single period; the mistake is treating "hit the revenue number" as this quarter's unifying story when it isn't actually what's different about this period.
- **The same fix generalizes across very different organizations** (hospitality, tech, healthcare) because the root cause it addresses — an absent shared context, not a personality problem — is structural, not industry-specific.

## 7. Bottom Line

Silos aren't a discipline problem or a personality problem — they're what happens by default when leadership never gives the organization one specific, shared, time-bound thing to move toward together. The fix isn't a mandate to collaborate; it's naming a real thematic goal, owning it jointly at the leadership level, and being disciplined about not letting a standard operating metric quietly take its place.

## 8. Glossary — Vocabulary Used in This Chapter

| Term / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Silo** | A departmental barrier that causes people who should be on the same team to work against each other. |
| **Thematic Goal** | A single, qualitative, time-bound organizational priority, shared and owned by the entire leadership team. |
| **Defining Objective** | A concrete, actionable objective that gives a thematic goal real shape. |
| **Standard Operating Objective** | An ongoing, necessary metric (revenue, quality, satisfaction) that should not be mistaken for a rallying cry. |
| **Rallying cry** | A goal compelling and specific enough to actually unify effort across departments, as opposed to routine metrics everyone already tracks. |
| **Compelling context** | A shared reason to work together specific enough to override each department's default incentive to optimize for itself. |
| **Fable (business)** | A narrative-driven business book that delivers its argument through a fictional story rather than direct exposition. |
