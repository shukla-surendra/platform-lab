# Case Study: The Alliance Tax (Forced to Pick a Side)

A tenth case study in this series. This one isn't about a mechanism targeted at one person — it's about what happens to everyone caught downstream of a war between two people with power, where genuine neutrality turns out not to be an option that's actually on offer.

## 1. The Situation

Two senior stakeholders — say a VP of Engineering and a VP of Product, or two directors with overlapping scope — entered a sustained conflict over strategy, resourcing, or territory. The conflict was never named openly at a company-wide level; it surfaced instead as competing asks, contradictory signals, and pointed questions in meetings.

Everyone whose work touched both stakeholders came under implicit pressure to align: attending one's meetings more visibly than the other's, agreeing more readily with one's framing, was read by both sides as a loyalty signal. An engineer who tried to stay genuinely neutral — giving honest technical input to both, declining to take a side — found that the neutrality itself got read as siding with the other camp, by both camps simultaneously.

## 2. Root-Cause Analysis

**Mechanism A — binary framing collapses neutrality into opposition.** Once a conflict is perceived by its participants as two-sided, any signal that isn't legible as support gets processed as opposition by default — the participants aren't evaluating third parties on a neutral scale, they're evaluating them relative to their own side. "Not with me" reads as "against me," regardless of the third party's actual position.

**Mechanism B — the cost of neutrality is immediate; the cost of picking a side is deferred.** Declining to align has a visible, immediate cost: both stakeholders individually cooler, less generous with opportunities. Picking a side has a real but deferred cost, contingent entirely on which side eventually wins and how the losing side later treats anyone who backed the winner. This asymmetry pressures people toward taking a side even with no substantive stake in the underlying conflict, purely because neutrality is punished with certainty and side-taking only probabilistically.

**Why it's hard to exit cleanly.** Because the underlying conflict is never named openly, there's no legitimate forum to simply say "I'm not taking a side in this" without that statement itself being read as a move within the conflict. Naming the dynamic is itself an action inside the dynamic — which is exactly what makes it difficult to step outside of unilaterally.

## 3. What the Individual Contributed

- **Engaging with the conflict's substance as if it were a legitimate technical question**, rather than recognizing early that it had become a political conflict in which technical merit was no longer the actual deciding factor.
- **Not naming the observed pressure to a trusted, genuinely neutral third party** — a mentor, HR, a skip-level outside either stakeholder's chain — who could help calibrate whether the read was accurate before the cost compounded further.

## 4. What the Organization Got Wrong

- **Allowing a sustained, consequential conflict between two senior stakeholders to remain unnamed and unmanaged at a level above both of them**, which pushed the cost of resolving it down onto everyone in both of their reporting chains instead.
- **No leadership norm explicitly protecting genuine neutrality.** A senior leader stating plainly, "no one below either of you is required to take a side in this, and I will treat pressure to do so as a problem," would remove the incentive structure that makes the alliance tax possible — but this requires someone above both stakeholders willing to name the conflict directly, which rarely happens on its own.

## 5. The Forward Protocol

1. **Recognize the shift from technical disagreement to political conflict as early as possible.** The tell: positions stop moving in response to evidence and start moving only in response to who's asking.
2. **Stick to observable, factual, scoped statements rather than framing-level opinions** when either side asks for input — "here's what the data shows for this specific question" is much harder to read as alignment than "I think approach A is the right call."
3. **Document requests from both sides neutrally, holding both to the same standard** — this protects against a later accusation of favoritism from either side and creates a record if the dynamic needs escalation.
4. **If pressure to align becomes explicit or costly, name it to someone above both conflicting parties**, if such a person exists and is safe to approach — the alliance tax specifically depends on the conflict staying unnamed above the level where it's being fought; naming it upward is the direct counter.
5. **Accept that some cost from at least one side may be unavoidable once a conflict has hardened into a binary framing.** The realistic goal is minimizing cost and avoiding actively worsening it, not achieving a costless neutrality the dynamic itself has made structurally unavailable.

## 6. Coaching Takeaway

In a binary conflict between people with power over you, neutrality is not a safe default — it's read as a position by both sides regardless of intent. The right response isn't finding a cleverer way to stay neutral; it's recognizing the conflict for what it is early, minimizing your surface area in its substance, and — if the cost becomes real — naming it to whoever actually has the standing to resolve the underlying conflict. No amount of local skill fixes a problem whose real cause sits one level above where it's being fought.

## 7. Glossary — Vocabulary Used in This Chapter

| Term / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Binary framing** | Perceiving or presenting a situation as having exactly two opposing sides, with no legitimate third position. |
| **Alliance tax** | The cost imposed on a third party for failing to visibly align with one side of a conflict between more powerful people. |
| **Legible (signal)** | A signal that is clearly interpretable by an observer as meaning something specific, as opposed to ambiguous or unreadable. |
| **Deferred cost** | A cost that isn't paid immediately but depends on a future, uncertain outcome. |
| **Probabilistic cost** | A cost that only materializes with some likelihood, rather than with certainty. |
| **Political conflict (vs. technical)** | A disagreement driven by power, territory, or incentives rather than by an evaluable question of technical merit. |
| **Scoped statement** | A statement limited to a specific, factual, checkable claim, avoiding broader framing-level opinions. |
| **Standing (to resolve)** | The organizational authority or position required to actually adjudicate or end a conflict. |
