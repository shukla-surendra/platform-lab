# Prerequisite Stacking and the Elsewhere Effect — Two Patterns That Sabotage Deep Work

Two behavioral patterns, distinct in mechanism but often found **hunting in pairs**: one postpones the hard task indefinitely by inserting an endless chain of "necessary" steps before it; the other makes sure that whatever activity *does* happen — study or rest, work or leisure — never gets full attention, because part of the mind is always **camped out** in the activity that isn't currently happening. This chapter names both precisely, traces the mechanism underneath each, and gives a remedy that addresses the mechanism rather than just the symptom.

## 1. Prerequisite Stacking (Sequencing as Avoidance)

### Definition

**Prerequisite stacking** is the habit of inserting "one more thing to prepare" between the present moment and a task that carries real evaluative risk — a mock interview, a timed test, a difficult conversation, a first draft shown to someone else. Each prerequisite is individually plausible ("I should really finish this chapter first"), which is exactly what makes the pattern **insidious** — it never announces itself as procrastination, because on paper it looks like diligence.

### The Mechanism

The task being avoided isn't avoided because it's unimportant — it's avoided because it produces an uncomfortable, evaluative feeling: the risk of discovering, in real time, that the current skill level falls short. Reading a book about the topic produces none of that discomfort; it feels like forward motion while remaining entirely inside the safety of **recognition** rather than **production** (`08_Interview_Communication/03_the_collectors_fallacy_fixing_prep_without_progress.md` covers the recognition/production distinction in depth).

Every time a new prerequisite gets inserted and the scary task gets pushed out one more day, two things happen simultaneously:
1. The anxiety about the task drops immediately — this is genuine, physiological relief.
2. That relief **reinforces** the exact behavior that produced it — inserting a prerequisite — the same way any behavior gets strengthened when it's followed by relief from discomfort (negative reinforcement, in the technical sense: a behavior that *removes* an aversive state gets repeated).

This is why the pattern rarely responds to willpower or self-criticism. It isn't a motivation deficit — the loop is actively rewarded, every single time it runs, by real anxiety reduction. Telling oneself to "just stop procrastinating" doesn't touch the loop; the loop needs to stop being the fastest available route to relief.

### How It Shows Up

- A "foundations first" book that never quite finishes, while the timed practice it was supposed to prepare for keeps getting rescheduled.
- A tool, template, or system being built *for* the task, which quietly becomes a more comfortable project than the task itself.
- Research and comparison-shopping ("which course is actually the best one") that expands to fill all the available time that should have gone to practice.
- A vague, unfalsifiable sense of "not ready yet" that never resolves into a specific, checkable condition — because if it were specific and checkable, it would eventually be satisfied, and the loop needs it to stay perpetually unsatisfied.

The **tell** is structural, not emotional: the chain of prerequisites never terminates in the scary task on its own. Left alone, it will always generate one more link. Termination only happens when something external forces it — a deadline, an interview actually being scheduled, someone else asking "so when's the real attempt happening?"

### Why It's Hard to Self-Correct

Because the felt experience from the inside is indistinguishable from genuine thoroughness. Genuine thoroughness and prerequisite stacking both *look like* "working hard on preparation." The only reliable way to tell them apart is to check whether the sequence has an end condition that was fixed **in advance**, before the anxiety had a chance to move the goalposts. Thoroughness has a pre-committed stopping point. Avoidance discovers a new stopping point every time the old one gets close.

### Remedy

**Fix the exposure date before fixing the preparation plan, not after.** Decide when the mock interview, the timed test, or the difficult conversation happens — put it on a calendar, ideally with another person involved so it isn't privately reschedulable — *before* deciding what "preparation" consists of. Every candidate prerequisite then has to justify itself against a date that's already fixed, rather than being free to push the date outward. This single reversal — date first, prerequisites second — removes the mechanism's only degree of freedom.

Secondary remedies:
- **Define "ready" in advance, as a specific and falsifiable condition**, not a feeling. "Ready" is not allowed to mean "when it doesn't feel scary anymore" — it should mean something checkable, like "having attempted three timed problems in this category," a bar that gets satisfied and stays satisfied.
- **Notice relief as a warning signal, not a green light.** The moment a new prerequisite gets added and a wave of relief follows, that relief is the tell that avoidance just happened, not confirmation that the right call was made.

## 2. The Elsewhere Effect (Context-Contingent Ought)

### Definition

The **Elsewhere Effect** describes a state in which the felt sense of "what I should be doing" is defined *relationally* — as whatever isn't currently happening — rather than *absolutely*, as a fixed standard independent of location. In its clearest form: while at home, no urge to study appears; once out, a conviction arrives that studying should be happening right now, and that it would be happening "if only" the present location were different. The standard is never satisfied, because it is not actually about studying — it is about being wherever the body currently is not.

This draws directly on **self-discrepancy theory** (Higgins, 1987), which distinguishes two kinds of internal mismatch:
- **Actual–ideal discrepancy** (a gap between how things are and how one *wishes* they were) tends to produce dejection-related emotion — disappointment, sadness.
- **Actual–ought discrepancy** (a gap between how things are and how one believes they *should* be, per some internalized standard or obligation) tends to produce agitation-related emotion — guilt, tension, restlessness.

The pattern described here is a textbook actual–ought discrepancy, with one unusual feature: the "ought" is not pinned to a fixed content (study X hours, cover Y material) but is pinned to *location relative to the present one*. That makes it structurally unsatisfiable — the ought-self, by definition, always occupies the other location, so arriving there would simply relocate the ought-self one step further away. This is functionally the same shape as the idiom **"the grass is always greener"**, except turned inward and moralized: not "the other option looks nicer" but "the other option is where I'm obligated to be."

### Why It Forms

A pattern like this rarely starts as an explicit belief — it tends to be assembled early, from an environment in which neither full effort nor full rest was ever clearly **sanctioned** without qualification. If working hard was met with "but what about X" and resting was met with "shouldn't you be doing Y," both states end up **contaminated** with a low background hum of obligation, and the mind never fully settles into either one. Over time this generalizes past the original context into any pairing of "here" and "elsewhere."

### The Cost

The pattern produces the worst outcome of the four theoretically possible combinations. Consider the options:

| | Fully present, working | Fully present, resting |
|---|---|---|
| **At the work location** | Real output | (n/a — wrong combination for illustration) |
| **At the rest location** | (n/a) | Real recovery |
| **The Elsewhere Effect** | Neither — attention split toward "elsewhere" in both cases | Neither — same |

The person experiencing it gets the discomfort of feeling perpetually behind **without** the compensating benefit of either actual output or actual recovery — an outcome closely related to the idiom **falling between two stools**: failing at both of two alternatives by trying to straddle both at once instead of committing to either. Divided attention doesn't average the two states; it tends to produce something worse than either state chosen fully.

### Distinguishing It From Related Patterns

- **The Zeigarnik effect** (unfinished tasks intrude on unrelated thinking) is task-specific and resolves once the task closes. The Elsewhere Effect is chronic and *relational* — it isn't attached to one open task, it's attached to "not-here" as a category, so completing any single task doesn't resolve it.
- **FOMO** is typically about a specific missed experience (a social event, an opportunity). This pattern is closer to an internally generated, duty-flavored FOMO — the missed thing is always "the responsible version of myself," not an external event.
- **Ordinary guilt** is usually anchored to a real, identifiable lapse. Here the ought is frequently untested against the record — "I would have studied if I'd been home" is a counterfactual claim, rarely checked against what actually happens on the days spent at home.

### Remedy

**Pre-commitment collapses the ambiguity the pattern feeds on.** If a specific block of time is fixed in advance as study time, then time outside that block is not "stolen" from an unstated, open-ended obligation — it is already-allocated non-study time, in full, and there is nothing left to feel behind on. The felt "ought" gets discharged by the decision itself, made once, rather than needing to be re-litigated in every subsequent moment.

Additional remedies, layered:
- **Cognitive defusion** (a core technique from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy): the goal is not to make the "I should be studying" thought disappear, but to change the relationship to it — noticing *"there's that thought again"* rather than treating the thought as a command that must be obeyed or resisted. A thought noticed and left alone loses most of its **hijacking** power over the current activity.
- **Single-context presence training** — short, deliberate blocks (start small, even ten minutes) spent fully inside one context, with the alternate context made inaccessible (phone away, no mental to-do list), treating each moment of the mind drifting "elsewhere" and being brought back as the actual unit of practice, not as a failure of the exercise. This is the same skill trained in attention/meditation practice, applied to a specific, named failure mode instead of generically.
- **Audit the ought against the real record.** The claim "I would have studied if I'd been home" is falsifiable — check the last ten times spent at home and see how many actually produced study. If the honest answer is "rarely," the guilt is attached to a **phantom** standard, not a real forfeited outcome, and naming that plainly tends to deflate it faster than arguing with it.

## 3. Why the Two Compound Each Other

Prerequisite stacking postpones the high-risk task indefinitely by always finding one more acceptable-looking substitute. The Elsewhere Effect then guarantees that even the substitute activity doesn't get full value, because attention is never fully **anchored** wherever it currently is — part of it is always reaching toward the task that got postponed, or toward whichever context isn't the current one. The net result is a **self-perpetuating** loop with no clean edges: no real progress on the avoided task (prerequisite stacking prevents it), and no real recovery from the substitute activity either (the Elsewhere Effect prevents that too) — output is lost on both sides, in perpetuity, until something structural interrupts the loop.

This is also why fixing only one half tends to underperform. Scheduling the scary task (the fix for prerequisite stacking) helps, but if attention still can't settle inside whatever block of time precedes it, that time gets **chased** rather than used. Both remedies are load-bearing together.

## 4. Combined Remedy Protocol

A short, practical sequence that applies both fixes at once:

1. **Fix the exposure date first.** Put the actual test, mock interview, or hard conversation on a calendar, with another person if possible, before deciding anything about preparation.
2. **Block the time between now and then into named, boundaried segments** — explicit work blocks and explicit rest/out blocks, each with a defined start and end.
3. **Treat each block as fully allocated once it's set.** Time inside a work block is not up for negotiation toward more prep browsing; time inside a rest block is not up for negotiation toward guilt about studying. The decision was already made when the block was created.
4. **When attention drifts toward "elsewhere" inside a block, notice it, name it, and return** — this is the rep, not a sign the system is failing.
5. **At the end of the week, check the record, not the feeling.** Did the scheduled exposure event actually happen? Did the work blocks produce real output? Did the rest blocks produce real recovery? Adjust block sizes based on that record, not based on how "ready" anything felt in the moment.

## 5. Glossary — Vocabulary Used in This Chapter

| Term / Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Hunting in pairs** | Two things (here, patterns) that tend to occur together, reinforcing each other. |
| **Camped out** | Settled into a state or position, often for longer than intended. |
| **Insidious** | Proceeding in a gradual, subtle way that is more harmful than it appears. |
| **Evaluative risk** | The risk specifically of being judged or assessed, as opposed to general difficulty. |
| **Negative reinforcement** | A behavior gets strengthened because it removes or reduces an unpleasant state (distinct from punishment, which suppresses behavior). |
| **Unfalsifiable** | Not able to be proven false or checked against evidence — a claim structured so it can never be tested. |
| **Moving the goalposts** | Changing the criteria for success partway through, usually to make success harder to reach. |
| **Self-discrepancy theory** | Higgins' psychological model describing emotional consequences of gaps between actual, ideal, and ought selves. |
| **Actual–ought discrepancy** | The gap between how things currently are and how one believes they are obligated to be; associated with guilt/agitation. |
| **Sanctioned** | Officially permitted or approved — used here for an activity being allowed without hidden qualification. |
| **Contaminate** | To spoil or taint something (here, an emotional state) by mixing in an unwanted element. |
| **The grass is always greener** | Idiom: the belief that other circumstances or choices are better than one's own, often without basis. |
| **Falling between two stools** | Idiom: failing to achieve either of two alternatives because of trying to pursue both at once. |
| **Phantom (standard/obligation)** | Something that feels real and binding but doesn't hold up when checked against actual evidence. |
| **Cognitive defusion** | An ACT technique of relating to a thought as "just a thought" rather than as literal truth or a command. |
| **Hijack (attention)** | To seize control of something — here, a thought taking over present-moment focus without permission. |
| **Anchored** | Firmly fixed or settled in place — used here for attention staying with the current activity. |
| **Self-perpetuating** | A process that sustains or renews itself without needing outside input. |
| **In perpetuity** | Forever; without an end point. |
| **Chased (time)** | Pursued anxiously rather than used calmly — time spent scrambling instead of settled. |
| **Load-bearing** | Structurally essential — removing it causes the whole thing to fail, borrowed here from architecture. |
