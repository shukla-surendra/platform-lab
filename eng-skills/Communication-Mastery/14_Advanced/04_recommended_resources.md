# Recommended Resources

Curated to run alongside the daily practice system (`10_Daily_Practice`), not as a replacement for it — per the testing-effect discussion in `01_Foundations/02`, consuming these resources builds recognition, not production skill. Pair every resource here with an active exercise from `11_Exercises` for it to actually change how you speak.

## Books — Structure and Frameworks

- **"The Pyramid Principle" — Barbara Minto.** The original source for the framework in `03_Explanation_Frameworks/02`. Dense and consulting-oriented, but the single best deep-dive on structuring complex written arguments.
- **"Made to Stick" — Chip Heath & Dan Heath.** The research behind why concrete, specific, story-shaped communication outperforms abstract communication — directly underpins `04_Technical_Storytelling`.
- **"The Minto Pyramid Principle" workbook editions** — for hands-on drilling if the main book feels too abstract on a first read.
- **"Thinking, Fast and Slow" — Daniel Kahneman.** Background on cognitive load and dual-process thinking that underlies the working-memory material in `01_Foundations/02`. Not a communication book directly, but explains the mechanism.
- **"Talk Like TED" — Carmine Gallo.** A practical breakdown of what makes technical/scientific talks land with general audiences — useful for the altitude-shifting skill in `01_Foundations/03`.
- **"Resonate" — Nancy Duarte.** Presentation structure specifically, strong on the narrative-arc material that complements `04_Technical_Storytelling`.
- **"Crucial Conversations" — Kerry Patterson et al.** The best available deep-dive on high-stakes disagreement, directly relevant to `05_Phrase_Library/03` and `09_Meeting_Communication`.
- **"The Manager's Path" — Camille Fournier.** Not a communication book per se, but excellent on the altitude and audience-calibration demands as engineers move toward Staff+/Principal scope.

## Books — Technical Writing (Transfers Directly to Verbal Structure)

- **"On Writing Well" — William Zinsser.** General-purpose clarity and cutting-the-fat writing advice that transfers almost directly to spoken explanation quality.
- **Google's technical writing courses / style guidance** — widely available material on writing clear technical documents; the "one idea per sentence" discipline transfers directly to spoken PREP delivery.

## Podcasts

- Engineering-leadership-focused podcasts that regularly feature Staff+/Principal engineers narrating real architecture decisions — listen specifically for how guests structure answers to "why did you do X," not just for the technical content. This is a direct application of the shadowing technique in `12_Recording_Analysis/01`.
- Podcasts built around case-study-style deep dives on real production incidents and postmortems — excellent source material for studying the incident-narrative shape in `04_Technical_Storytelling/02` as actually practiced by others.

## Conference Talks — What to Watch For, Not Just What to Watch

When watching any recorded conference talk (AWS re:Invent, KubeCon, Databricks Data + AI Summit, or similar), don't just absorb the technical content — actively analyze the *structure*, using this lens:

- **Where in the talk does the speaker state their core point?** (First 2 minutes, or buried?)
- **How do they handle audience-altitude shifts** between the opening framing and the deep technical section?
- **What's their pause behavior** — do they use silence deliberately, or fill every gap?
- **How do they open** — do they use an SCQA-style situation/complication, or jump straight to the answer?
- **How do they handle the live Q&A** — does their answer-first discipline hold up under an unexpected question, or does structure degrade under pressure?

This turns any talk into a structured study exercise rather than passive viewing — pair with the shadowing exercise in `12_Recording_Analysis/01`.

**Categories of talks worth actively studying this way:**
- Principal/Distinguished Engineer keynotes from major cloud providers' annual conferences (AWS re:Invent, Google Cloud Next, Microsoft Build/Ignite) — these are usually the most polished examples of translating deep technical work into a broad-audience narrative.
- Postmortem/incident-retrospective talks specifically (many companies publish these at engineering conferences) — direct application of `04_Technical_Storytelling/02`.
- System design deep-dive talks from large-scale infrastructure teams — study both the content and, separately, the verbal-diagram-description technique from `07_Architecture_Communication/02`.

## TED / TEDx Talks

Even though most TED talks aren't engineering-specific, they remain some of the most heavily-optimized examples of the exact skills this repository trains: answer-first framing, concrete storytelling with genuine tension, and calibrated pacing with deliberate pauses. Watch a handful specifically for structure, using the same analytical lens described above — content relevance to engineering matters far less than structural quality for this purpose.

## YouTube Channels and Recorded Talk Archives

- Official conference channels for AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Databricks, and the CNCF (KubeCon) — search for "keynote" or "deep dive" sessions from Principal/Staff-level speakers specifically, since title/role is a reasonable proxy for communication polish at that level.
- Engineering blogs and their associated talk archives from large-scale tech companies that publish detailed postmortems and architecture deep-dives — read the written postmortem, then find the associated talk if one exists, and compare how the same content changes shape between written and spoken form (directly relevant to `06_Project_Presentation` and `09_Meeting_Communication`).

## Great Speakers to Study (By Pattern, Not Just By Name)

Rather than a fixed name list (which dates quickly), look for speakers who exhibit these specific patterns, and study *those patterns specifically*:

- **The "answer in the first 10 seconds" pattern** — common among Principal Engineers doing internal tech talks at large tech companies; look for talks explicitly framed as "lessons learned" or "postmortem," which structurally force this discipline.
- **The "one analogy, fully committed" pattern** — speakers who pick a single strong analogy and return to it throughout a talk, rather than switching metaphors — study how they handle the analogy's edge cases when it doesn't perfectly apply.
- **The "comfortable with silence" pattern** — noticeable in experienced conference keynote speakers; time their pauses if you can and notice how much longer they are than feels natural to attempt yourself at first.

## Courses

- Any structured technical-writing or "explaining technical concepts" course offered internally at large tech companies (often shared publicly as blog posts or open training materials) — these are frequently built by the same technical-writing teams responsible for company-wide documentation standards and tend to align closely with the Pyramid Principle material in `03_Explanation_Frameworks/02`.
- Public-speaking-specific courses (Toastmasters remains a widely available, low-cost, high-repetition option specifically for building comfort with live delivery and pacing — the repetition and real audience feedback it provides pairs well with the solo recording practice in `12_Recording_Analysis`).

## How to Use This List Without It Becoming Passive Consumption

Apply the **1:3 rule**: for every hour spent consuming a resource from this list, spend at least three times that actively practicing (`10_Daily_Practice`, `11_Exercises`) — using what you just watched/read as direct input into your next recorded rep, not as a standalone activity. A talk you watched but never applied is entertainment, not training; per the testing-effect research in `01_Foundations/02`, only the applied half of that ratio reliably builds durable skill.
