# Article

There are **two types of articles**:

1. **Definite article** → *the*

2. **Indefinite articles** → *a* and *an*

### **1\. Indefinite Articles – a / an**

We use **a** or **an** when talking about something **non-specific** or when mentioning it **for the first time**.

* **a** → used before words that start with a consonant sound.

* **an** → used before words that start with a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u).

✅ Examples:

* I saw **a** cat in the garden. (not a specific cat, just any cat)

* She bought **an** apple from the market.

* He is **a** doctor.

⚠️ Note: It depends on **sound**, not spelling.

* **an hour** (because "hour" starts with a vowel sound /au/)

* **a university** (because "university" starts with a consonant sound /juː/)

### **2\. Definite Article – the**

We use **the** when talking about something **specific** or something that the listener/reader already knows.

* I saw **the** cat in your garden yesterday. (a specific cat, already known)

* Please close **the** door. (the one we both know)

* She is **the** best singer in our class.

Other uses of **the**:

* With unique things: **the sun, the moon, the Earth**

* With superlatives: **the tallest building, the best movie**

* With ordinal numbers: **the first day, the second chapter**

* With countries having plural names or “kingdom/republic”: **the United States, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom**

### **3\. When NO Article is Used (Zero Article)**

Sometimes, we don’t use any article.

* I like **milk**. (uncountable, general sense)

* She studies **mathematics**. (subject)

* He goes to **school** every day. (institution used for its primary purpose)

* We had dinner at **home**.

But:

* I bought **a milk** → ❌ (wrong, unless you mean "a bottle of milk").

### **4\. Quick Comparison**

* I saw **a** dog. (any dog)

* I saw **the** dog that bit you yesterday. (specific dog)

* I like **dogs**. (general sense, no article)

### **Spoken Sentences You Can Practice:**

1. “I need **an** umbrella because it looks like it might rain.”

2. “Can you pass me **the** salt, please?”

3. “She bought **a** new laptop yesterday.”

4. “We had lunch at **home** instead of going out.”

5. “I want to visit **the** Taj Mahal one day.”

👉 Now, try practicing aloud with real-life objects around you. For example:

* “This is **a** pen.”

* “This is **the** pen I always use.”

* “I like **pens** in general.”

### **🔑 How to Get the Hang of Articles:**

1. **Think Specific vs. General**

   * If it’s general → no article.

   * If it’s one of many → *a / an*.

   * If it’s specific or already known → *the*.

2. Example with *music*:

   * “I listen to **music**.” (general, no article → talking about music in general)

   * “I listen to **the music**.” (specific → maybe the music in this room, or the one currently playing)

2. **Listen and Repeat**  
    Try shadowing (repeating immediately after hearing) short English sentences from movies, podcasts, or even YouTube. Over time, you’ll notice patterns like:

   * “I saw a car.”

   * “The car was red.”

   * “Cars are expensive.”

3. **Practice with Pairs**  
    Use one word in 3 ways:

   * “I bought **a book**.” (any book)

   * “I liked **the book** you gave me.” (specific)

   * “I love **books**.” (general, no article)

4. 👉 Do this daily with random objects: pen, phone, chair, laptop, coffee, etc.

4. **Don’t Overthink in the Moment**  
    Articles are small words. If you miss one while speaking, people still understand you. It’s better to focus on **clarity** first. With practice, correctness will follow.

### **💡 Emphasis when using articles**

When you *do* use an article, the stress can change the meaning slightly:

* “I listen to **music**.” → general habit.

* “I listen to **the music**.” → emphasizing a particular piece that we both know.

* “I bought **a book**.” → one book, not many.

* “I bought **the book**.” → the special/known book.

### **Spoken Sentences to Practice:**

1. “I usually listen to **music** when I’m working.”

2. “Please lower **the music**, it’s too loud.”

3. “I had **a coffee** this morning.”

4. “She gave me **the coffee** I ordered.”

5. “I don’t usually drink **coffee** in the evening.”

👉 Try saying these aloud, and notice the small difference in meaning when you add *the*.

# Conditional Sentences

Conditional sentences have two parts: the **if-clause** (the condition) and the **main clause** (the result). English has four core types, each tied to a different sense of how *real* or *likely* the situation is. Getting the type right matters more in professional English than it seems — it's how you signal certainty vs. speculation vs. regret.

### **1\. Zero Conditional — general truths / facts**

* **Form**: If \+ present simple, ... present simple.

* **Usage**: Something that is *always* true — facts, habits, rules, science, processes.

✅ Examples:

* "If you heat water to 100°C, it boils."

* "If the build fails, the pipeline sends an alert automatically."

* "If I skip breakfast, I get a headache by noon."

💡 Both clauses are present tense — there's no "future" here, because it's not a one-off prediction, it's a rule.

### **2\. First Conditional — real / likely future**

* **Form**: If \+ present simple, ... will \+ base verb.

* **Usage**: A realistic condition about the future — something that could genuinely happen.

✅ Examples:

* "If the tests pass, we'll deploy tonight."

* "If you send the doc by Friday, I'll review it over the weekend."

* "If traffic is bad, I'll be a few minutes late."

⚠️ **Common mistake**: don't use "will" in the if-clause. Not *"If it will rain, I will take an umbrella"* — say *"If it rains, I will take an umbrella."* The if-clause stays in present tense even though it's about the future.

### **3\. Second Conditional — hypothetical present/future, unlikely or untrue now**

* **Form**: If \+ past simple, ... would \+ base verb.

* **Usage**: An imagined situation that's unlikely, purely hypothetical, or contrary to current fact. Also used to soften opinions/suggestions in meetings.

✅ Examples:

* "If I had more bandwidth this sprint, I'd take on that refactor." (I don't — this is hypothetical.)

* "If she were the tech lead, she'd probably restructure the whole service." (She isn't.)

* "If we redesigned the schema now, we'd save time later." (A speculative suggestion, not a plan yet.)

💡 Note "were" instead of "was" after *I/he/she/it* in careful/formal English ("If I were you...") — very common in spoken feedback and advice.

### **4\. Third Conditional — hypothetical past, regret or a different outcome**

* **Form**: If \+ past perfect, ... would have \+ past participle.

* **Usage**: Talking about a past situation that didn't happen, and its imagined (different) result. Common in retrospectives / incident postmortems.

✅ Examples:

* "If we had caught this in code review, we wouldn't have shipped the bug."

* "If I had known about the outage earlier, I would have paged the on-call engineer sooner."

* "The launch would have gone smoother if we had tested on staging first."

💡 This is the conditional you reach for in a blameless postmortem: describing what *would* have prevented an incident, without necessarily assigning blame.

### **5\. Mixed Conditionals (brief)**

Sometimes a past condition has a *present* result, or a present condition has a *past* result — these mix the second and third conditional forms.

* "If I hadn't taken that job, I wouldn't be living here now." (past condition → present result)

* "If she were more detail-oriented, she would have caught that typo in the report." (present condition → past result)

You don't need to actively produce these often, but recognizing them when you hear them helps.

### **6\. Quick comparison table**

| Type | If-clause | Main clause | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present simple | present simple | always true |
| First | present simple | will \+ base | realistic future |
| Second | past simple | would \+ base | unlikely/hypothetical now |
| Third | past perfect | would have \+ past participle | unreal past, different outcome |

### **7\. Ready-to-use spoken sentences (workplace situations)**

* "If the migration goes well, we'll roll it out to the rest of the fleet next week." (1st)

* "If I were leading this project, I'd push the deadline by a week." (2nd — softened opinion)

* "If we had load-tested this earlier, we would have caught the bottleneck before launch." (3rd — postmortem)

* "If a request times out, the client retries automatically." (0 — how the system behaves)

* "If you're free later, I'd like to walk you through the design." (1st — everyday scheduling)

✅ Practice aloud (say each type out loud with your own real example):

* Zero: "If \_\_\_\_\_, \_\_\_\_\_." (a fact about your codebase, team, or daily routine)

* First: "If \_\_\_\_\_, I'll \_\_\_\_\_." (something realistic you'll do this week)

* Second: "If I \_\_\_\_\_, I would \_\_\_\_\_." (something hypothetical, not currently true)

* Third: "If we had \_\_\_\_\_, we would have \_\_\_\_\_." (a past decision and its imagined different outcome)

# Frequency Adverbs

### **📌 Frequency Adverbs – Placement Rules**

1. **Before the main verb** (but after the subject)

   * Example: *I **always** wake up early.*

   * Example: *She **never** eats junk food.*

2. **After the verb "to be" (am, is, are, was, were)**

   * Example: *He **is always** late.*

   * Example: *They **are often** tired.*

3. **Between an auxiliary verb and the main verb**

   * Example: *You **can always** call me.*

   * Example: *We **have often** discussed this.*

4. **At the beginning or end of a sentence** (less common, for emphasis)

   * Example: *Sometimes, I forget my keys.*

   * Example: *I go jogging **rarely**.*

### **📌 Frequency Scale (most to least)**

* Always (100%)

* Usually / Generally (80–90%)

* Often / Frequently (60–70%)

* Sometimes / Occasionally (40–50%)

* Rarely / Seldom (10–20%)

* Never (0%)

### **✅ Common Sentences You Can Practice**

* *I **always** drink coffee in the morning.*

* *She **usually** takes the bus to work.*

* *We **often** go out for dinner on weekends.*

* *He **rarely** watches TV at night.*

* *They **never** complain about the weather.*

### **💡 Phrases for Conversations**

* "I **usually** prefer tea, but sometimes I go for coffee."

* "On the other hand, I **never** skip breakfast."

* "For example, I **often** work late on Fridays."

* "That being said, I **rarely** stay up past midnight."

👉 Try speaking these sentences out loud. It will help you get used to the rhythm of adverb placement.

# Has / Have Had (Present Perfect)

**"Has had / have had"** is the present perfect of the verb *have*. It describes something that started in the past and is either still true now or still relevant now.

## A More Advanced Structure: "Have Had To"

* **I / You / We / They → have had**
* **He / She / It → has had**

Example: *I **have had** this laptop for five years.* / *She **has had** a headache since morning.*

Here, *have/has* is the present-perfect helper and *had* is the past participle of *have* — together they signal that the situation started in the past and continues to matter now.

## Common Situations in Spoken English

**Talking about experience:**
*I have had many interviews this year.* / *We have had similar issues in our system before.*

**Talking about something recent, with no specific time given:**
*I have had a long day.* / *We have had several production failures recently.*

**Talking about a continuing situation (something that started earlier and is still true):**
*I have had this phone since 2020.* / *Our team has had this architecture for a long time.*

**Talking about continuous possession** — got it in the past, still have it now:
*I have had this laptop for five years* means: I got the laptop five years ago, and I still have it.

## Difference from Simple Past

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **I had** a meeting yesterday. | Simple past — a specific, finished point in time. |
| **I have had** many meetings today. | Present perfect — past, but still relevant right now. |
| **I have a laptop.** | Simple present possession, right now. |
| **I had a laptop.** | Past possession — may not have it anymore. |
| **I have had this laptop for five years.** | Continuous possession — got it in the past, still have it. |

## Very Common Spoken Pattern

In conversation, people almost always contract it:

* **I have had → I've had**
* **We have had → We've had**
* **He has had → He's had**

*"Have you had lunch?" / "I've had enough of this issue." / "We've had similar bugs before."*

## Natural Spoken Sentences You Can Use

* "We have had similar issues in the past, so we should investigate the logs first."
* "I have had some experience working with this architecture."
* "Have you had a chance to look at the logs?"
* "That being said, we have had this problem before."
* "For example, we have had this failure in staging earlier."
* "On the other hand, we have had stable deployments for the last few weeks."

## Useful Connecting Phrases

* **For example:** "For example, we've had this failure in staging earlier."
* **On the other hand:** "On the other hand, we've had stable deployments for the last few weeks."
* **That being said:** "That being said, we've had a few incidents recently."

*"Have had to"* (present perfect of *have to*) is very common in technical discussions and incident calls — different from plain *"had to"*:

* *"I have had to restart the cluster several times."* — this has happened repeatedly, up to and including now.
* *"I had to restart the cluster."* — a single, specific past event.

# Rather

It helps the speaker **clarify, compare, correct, or soften a statement** so the listener understands better.

## **1\. Main meanings of “rather” in explanations**

### **a) To correct or refine what you just said**

* *"It’s not learning like a human, rather it’s pattern recognition."*

* This helps the audience avoid misunderstanding.

### **b) To show preference**

* *"Rather than storing all the data, the system only keeps key features."*

* Used when comparing two options.

### **c) To express degree (moderately)**

* *"The model is rather complex for a beginner."* (more common in British English speech)

## **2\. Why teachers and presenters use it often**

* It **guides thinking**: The listener follows how the speaker adjusts or redirects the idea.

* It **makes contrasts clear**: Good for explaining “A vs. B”.

* It **sounds polite**: Softer than bluntly saying *“No, that’s wrong.”*

## **3\. In an AI presentation**

You might hear:

* *"The AI isn’t thinking, rather it’s processing inputs based on algorithms."*

* *"We chose a smaller dataset rather than a huge one to reduce training time."*

* *"It’s rather difficult to interpret these results without context."*

# Until vs By

### **1\. “Until”**

* **Meaning**: Something continues *up to a point in time* (but not after).

* **Usage**: Focuses on the *duration* of time.

✅ Examples:

* “I’ll wait **until** 6 p.m.” (I’ll keep waiting, but after 6 p.m. I’ll stop.)

* “The shop is open **until** 9 p.m.” (It closes after 9 p.m.)

* “She didn’t speak **until** he arrived.” (She was silent before, but started speaking at that moment.)

### **2\. “By”**

* **Meaning**: *Not later than a specific time*; deadline.

* **Usage**: Focuses on the *end point / deadline*, not duration.

* “Please finish the report **by** tomorrow.” (Deadline: tomorrow.)

* “He will arrive **by** 5 p.m.” (He may come earlier, but not later than 5.)

* “Can you call me **by** lunchtime?” (Deadline: before or at lunchtime.)

### **3\. Common Confusion**

* **Until** \= continuing action

* **By** \= deadline or latest time

💡 Compare:

* “I’ll be in the office **until** 6.” (I stay there up to 6, then leave.)

* “I’ll finish my work **by** 6.” (The work will be completed no later than 6.)

### **4\. Your Sentence:**

You said: *“I have call by then only.”*  
 👉 More natural alternatives:

* “I have a call **until then**.” (Meaning: the call will last up to that time.)

* “I have a call **by then**.” (Meaning: the call is scheduled to happen at or before that time.)

* “I’ll be on a call **till then**.” (Casual, spoken English.)

* “I have a call going on **until then**.” (Clearer for ongoing call.)

### **5\. Ready-to-use Spoken Sentences (daily situations)**

* “I can’t leave, I’m on a call **until 5 p.m.**”

* “Can you send me the details **by tomorrow morning**?”

* “The meeting will continue **until lunchtime**.”

* “Please reply **by the end of the day**.”

* “I’ll stay here **until you’re ready**.”

✨ **Tip for you**:  
 If you want to express *your call is happening and will last until that point*, say:  
 👉 “I’ll be on a call until then.”  
 👉 “My call will go on till then.”  
 👉 “I’ll be busy with a call until that time.”

✅ Practice aloud (so it flows in real conversation):

* “I’ll be on a call until then.”

* “Can you share the update by then?”

* “I’ll stay in the meeting until 6.”

* “The document should be ready by tomorrow.”

# Used To (Past Habits and States)

### **1\. “Used to” (for past habits or states)**

* We use **“used to \+ base verb (first form)”** to talk about things that happened regularly in the past but **don’t happen now**.

  * Example: *I used to play cricket every evening.* (habit in the past)

  * Example: *She used to live in Delhi.* (past state, not anymore)

👉 Notice: after “used to,” we always use the **first form** of the verb, not \-ing or \-ed.  
 ✔ Correct: *I used to watch movies.*  
 ❌ Wrong: *I used to watched movies.*

### **2\. When “used to” is NOT about habits**

Sometimes it’s about a **past state** or **situation**, not a repeated action.

* *There used to be a park here.* (past existence/state)

* *He used to be very shy.* (not about habit, but personality in the past)

### **3\. Practice Sentences**

Try saying these aloud:

1. *I used to wake up early, but now I sleep late.*

2. *My grandparents used to live in the village.*

3. *There used to be a cinema hall near my school.*

4. *She used to be very quiet, but now she’s more talkative.*

5. *We used to go on road trips every summer.*

### **4\. Common Spoken Phrases**

Here are some natural ones you can use in conversation:

* *I used to do that all the time, but not anymore.*

* *There used to be a shop on this street, remember?*

* *I used to be really into video games, but I don’t play much now.*

* *It used to take me an hour to get to work, now it’s just 20 minutes.*

👉 **Pro tip for fluency**:  
 When you want to compare past and present, *“used to”* is perfect. You can also use connecting phrases:

* *Back then…*

* *But these days…*

* *On the other hand…*

Example: *I used to drink a lot of coffee, but these days I prefer tea.*

# Used To = Accustomed To

*"Used to"* has a second, unrelated meaning: **accustomed to / familiar with something**, not a past habit.

### **1\. “Used to” \= accustomed to / familiar with**

Here, *“used to”* means **comfortable with something** (not strange anymore).  
 The structure is different:  
 👉 *be used to \+ noun / pronoun / verb-ing*

Examples:

* *I am used to the cold weather.* (= I’m accustomed to it)

* *She is used to waking up early.* (= it’s normal for her now)

* *They are used to city life.* (= they’re comfortable with it)

### **2\. Difference from “used to \+ verb (first form)”**

* **Used to \+ verb (first form)** → past habit/state  
   *I used to play football.* (but not now)

* **Be used to \+ noun/verb-ing** → accustomed to (present or past, not about a habit)  
   *I am used to playing football in hot weather.* (= it doesn’t bother me)

### **3\. Practice Sentences**

Try saying these aloud:

1. *I am used to drinking black coffee without sugar.*

2. *He is used to driving in heavy traffic.*

3. *We are used to working late during project deadlines.*

4. *She wasn’t used to the spicy food at first, but now she loves it.*

5. *Are you used to the new office environment?*

### **4\. Common Spoken Phrases**

* *I’m used to it now, so it doesn’t bother me anymore.*

* *She’s not used to speaking in front of big groups.*

* *You’ll get used to it after a while.*

* *At first it was difficult, but I’m used to it now.*

👉 Quick **connecting phrase practice**:

* *At first… but now I’m used to…*

* *On the other hand, I wasn’t used to…*

* *For example, I’m used to…*

# Used To — Negative and Interrogative Forms

## **1\. Negative form (past habit/state)**

👉 *didn’t use to \+ verb (first form)*  
 Notice: after “didn’t,” we drop the “d” (not “didn’t used to”).

Examples:

* *I didn’t use to like vegetables, but now I eat them every day.*

* *She didn’t use to watch TV much.*

* *They didn’t use to live here.*

## **2\. Interrogative form (past habit/state)**

👉 *Did … use to \+ verb (first form)?*

* *Did you use to play cricket after school?*

* *Did he use to travel a lot?*

* *Where did they use to live?*

## **3\. Negative / Interrogative with “accustomed to” meaning**

Here we use the verb *be* (am/is/are/was/were).

* Negative:  
   *I’m not used to waking up early.*  
   *She wasn’t used to spicy food.*

* Interrogative:  
   *Are you used to the new office environment?*  
   *Was he used to driving in traffic?*

## **4\. Practice Sentences (speak these aloud)**

1. *I didn’t use to drink tea, but now I love it.*

2. *Did you use to go to school by bus?*

3. *We didn’t use to have smartphones when I was a kid.*

4. *Are you used to speaking in English every day?*

5. *She wasn’t used to city life when she first moved here.*

## **5\. Common Spoken Phrases**

* *I didn’t use to like this, but now I do.*

* *Did you use to live around here?*

* *I’m not used to this kind of weather.*

* *Are you used to working late hours?*

👉 **Tip for fluency**:  
 When you hear “didn’t use to” or “did you use to,” focus on the *use to* without “d,” because “did” already marks the past.
