Postgres job queue: load, sizing and per-tenant limits

A case study of the job management app in postgres_queue_lab/ (web UI, workers with heartbeats and acks, all on one Postgres). How much work does it give the database, what size of instance does it need for a given number of jobs and workers, and can it limit each tenant's queue? Every cost on this page was measured on the running app, not estimated from docs.

1. Short answers

How much pressure does it put on Postgres?

Less than you might think per job, and more than you'd think per worker. A job's whole life (submit, claim, ack, 3 history rows) costs about 2.5 KB of WAL and 3 commits. A laptop container pushed ~1,550 jobs/s. The steady cost is heartbeats: every busy worker writes twice a second, even when no new jobs arrive. As built (1 job per process, 3 connections each), connections run out long before CPU or disk: 100 jobs/s of 5-second jobs needs ~715 workers and ~2,150 connections.

What instance does it need?

With the tuned worker design (many jobs per process, batched heartbeats every 10s): up to ~10 jobs/s fits a 2 vCPU / 8 GB instance; ~100 jobs/s a 4 vCPU / 16 GB one with ~3,000 IOPS; ~1,000 jobs/s needs 8–16 vCPU / 32–64 GB with provisioned IOPS and a database of its own, and is where a dedicated broker starts to make sense. Disk and WAL volume, connections and vacuum decide the size, not CPU. Use the calculator for your own numbers.

Can it rate-limit per tenant?

Yes, in Postgres alone, inside the claim transaction: a concurrency cap (max running per tenant), a token bucket (max job starts per second, with burst), a backlog quota (max queued, rejected at submit with HTTP 429), and fair ordering so a noisy tenant can't starve the others. A tested prototype is in tenant_limits.sql: with 20 workers racing, every limit held exactly. See section 7.

Should it share the application's Postgres? When is a broker needed?

Share it at first: you get jobs that commit atomically with app data, at almost no extra cost. Move it off once it's more than ~20–40% of the instance's WAL or app latency rises with queue bursts (section 10). Add a broker at roughly 1,000–2,000 jobs/s sustained, or earlier for bursts, ~10 ms pickup, very short jobs or fan-out (section 11). Below ~100 jobs/s a broker mostly adds people cost; around 1,000 jobs/s the infrastructure costs cross (section 12). Brokers keep messages in their own log or Raft storage, not a database, so job status and history still live in Postgres (section 13).

Does any of this need a Postgres extension?

No. Everything uses core Postgres. Two are worth having anyway: pg_stat_statements to see query cost in production (all managed services offer it), and optionally pg_cron to run retention deletes.

Why a busy worker writes 564 bytes a second

The WAL breakdown for 15 seconds of two busy workers showed where the bytes go: per heartbeat, one Heap/UPDATE for the job row, one Heap/HOT_UPDATE for the worker row, two commits, a row-lock record, and three B-tree index inserts (90 in 15s, the largest item). The job heartbeat changes locked_until, which is indexed by jobs_lease. When an UPDATE changes an indexed column, Postgres can't do a HOT (heap-only tuple) update, so it writes a new entry into every index on the table.

Dropping jobs_lease fixed it: 34 of 36 job updates became HOT, index inserts went to zero, and WAL fell 36%. Reclaiming expired leases still finds its rows quickly through the (status, id) index, because only running jobs are scanned. Fewer index writes also means less index bloat for vacuum to clean up.

Measurement trap. The first change to each page after a checkpoint writes the whole 8 KB page to WAL (a "full page write"). My first heartbeat measurement ran right after a CHECKPOINT and reported ~2 KB per heartbeat, four times the true steady-state figure. Measure over a window that doesn't start at a checkpoint, or inspect record types with pg_walinspect.

5. Sizing: jobs, workers, connections, WAL

The two numbers that drive everything are the arrival rate λ (jobs per second) and the average duration D. By Little's law, the number of jobs running at once is C = λ × D. Workers are sized for C with ~30% headroom. The costs from section 3 then give the load:

running jobs      C        = λ × D
worker slots      S        = C / 0.7                       (30% headroom for bursts and retries)
processes         P        = S / jobs_per_process          (as built: 1)
connections                = P × conns_per_process + web pool (10)
commits/s                  ≈ 3λ + job_heartbeat_statements/s + P / heartbeat_interval
WAL bytes/s                ≈ 2,500 λ + (C / heartbeat_interval) × (416, or 214 if HOT) + (P / heartbeat_interval) × 148
storage                    ≈ λ × 86,400 × retention_days × (1.2 KB + payload)

As built: 1 job per process, 3 connections each, heartbeat every 1s, current indexes. Retention is the biggest storage lever: at 1,000 jobs/s keep a day or two in Postgres and archive the rest to object storage.

Two things stand out. The job lifecycle cost (2.5 KB, 3 commits) is the same in both designs, and at 1,000 jobs/s it alone is ~2.5 MB/s of WAL, more than half of what the laptop wrote at its ceiling. And in the as-built design, heartbeats and connections, not jobs, are most of the load: tuning cuts commits 5× and connections 50× while doing the same work.

6. Which instance to pick

A queue's working set is small (the queued and running rows plus the top of a few indexes), so it rarely needs much memory or CPU. It needs fast, steady commits (disk latency and IOPS), headroom for WAL (disk, replication, backups), and enough connections. Use these as starting points, then load-test with your real job mix:

Ballpark connection limits: managed Postgres usually sets max_connections from memory, in the low hundreds for 8–16 GB instances. Whatever the limit, aim to keep active connections near 2–4× the vCPU count; more connections past that make things slower, not faster.

7. Per-tenant rate limits and quotas

Yes, and it fits naturally: a claim is already a transaction, so the limits can be checked and charged in that same transaction, with no extra infrastructure. "Limit a tenant" can mean four different things, and you usually want all four:

LimitQuestion it answersWhere it's enforcedWhen exceeded
Concurrency capHow many of this tenant's jobs may run at once?ClaimJobs wait in the queue
Start rate (token bucket)How many jobs per second may start, with what burst?ClaimJobs wait in the queue
Backlog quotaHow many jobs may be waiting?SubmitRejected: HTTP 429
Fair shareWhose job runs next when several tenants are waiting?Claim ordering—

Without these, the claim is "oldest job first" across everyone: a tenant who submits 10,000 jobs makes every other tenant wait behind them.

Schema

ALTER TABLE jobqueue.jobs ADD COLUMN tenant_id text NOT NULL DEFAULT 'default';
CREATE INDEX jobs_tenant_ready   ON jobqueue.jobs (tenant_id, priority DESC, run_after, id) WHERE status = 'queued';
CREATE INDEX jobs_tenant_running ON jobqueue.jobs (tenant_id) WHERE status = 'running';

CREATE TABLE jobqueue.tenants (
  tenant_id      text PRIMARY KEY,
  max_running    int     NOT NULL DEFAULT 5,       -- concurrency cap
  rate_per_sec   numeric NOT NULL DEFAULT 10,      -- token bucket: job starts per second
  burst          numeric NOT NULL DEFAULT 10,      -- bucket size
  max_queued     int     NOT NULL DEFAULT 10000,   -- backlog quota, checked at submit
  tokens         numeric NOT NULL DEFAULT 10,      -- bucket state
  refilled_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  last_served_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT '-infinity'   -- for round-robin fairness
);

The claim, in four steps

CREATE FUNCTION jobqueue.claim(p_worker text, p_lease int DEFAULT 30) RETURNS SETOF jobqueue.jobs
LANGUAGE plpgsql AS $$
DECLARE t jobqueue.tenants; j jobqueue.jobs; avail numeric;
BEGIN
  -- 1. Expired leases first. They already count as running for their tenant, so no limit applies.
  RETURN QUERY UPDATE jobqueue.jobs SET attempts = attempts + 1, locked_by = p_worker,
                      locked_until = now() + make_interval(secs => p_lease)
    WHERE id = (SELECT id FROM jobqueue.jobs WHERE status = 'running' AND locked_until < now()
                FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1) RETURNING *;
  IF FOUND THEN RETURN; END IF;

  -- 2. Fairness + rate: the least recently served tenant that has a due job and a token.
  --    Locking its row serialises claims for that tenant; other workers SKIP it and serve someone else.
  SELECT * INTO t FROM jobqueue.tenants tn
  WHERE least(burst, tokens + extract(epoch FROM clock_timestamp() - refilled_at) * rate_per_sec) >= 1
    AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM jobqueue.jobs q
                WHERE q.tenant_id = tn.tenant_id AND q.status = 'queued' AND q.run_after <= now())
  ORDER BY last_served_at
  FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1;
  IF NOT FOUND THEN RETURN; END IF;

  -- 3. Concurrency cap. A new statement, so it sees every claim committed before we got the lock,
  --    and no one else can claim for this tenant until we commit. Finishing jobs only lower the count.
  IF (SELECT count(*) FROM jobqueue.jobs WHERE tenant_id = t.tenant_id AND status = 'running') >= t.max_running THEN
    UPDATE jobqueue.tenants SET last_served_at = clock_timestamp() WHERE tenant_id = t.tenant_id;  -- rotate past it
    RETURN;
  END IF;

  -- 4. Claim the tenant's next job, then spend a token.
  UPDATE jobqueue.jobs SET status = 'running', attempts = attempts + 1, locked_by = p_worker,
         locked_until = now() + make_interval(secs => p_lease)
  WHERE id = (SELECT id FROM jobqueue.jobs WHERE tenant_id = t.tenant_id AND status = 'queued' AND run_after <= now()
              ORDER BY priority DESC, run_after, id FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1)
  RETURNING * INTO j;
  IF NOT FOUND THEN RETURN; END IF;
  avail := least(t.burst, t.tokens + extract(epoch FROM clock_timestamp() - t.refilled_at) * t.rate_per_sec);
  UPDATE jobqueue.tenants SET tokens = avail - 1, refilled_at = clock_timestamp(), last_served_at = clock_timestamp()
  WHERE tenant_id = t.tenant_id;
  RETURN NEXT j;
END $$;

The details that make it correct under concurrency:

  • No running counter to drift. The cap is checked by counting running jobs through the small partial index (at most max_running entries). A counter column would need every code path (ack, nack, cancel, crash, reclaim) to decrement it exactly once; a count can't go wrong.
  • The tenant row lock is what makes the count safe. Every claim for a tenant holds its row, so two workers can't both see "2 running, cap 3" and both start one. In READ COMMITTED each PL/pgSQL statement takes a fresh snapshot, so the count in step 3 sees all earlier claims.
  • SKIP LOCKED on the tenant row means workers don't queue up behind a busy tenant; they serve the next one.
  • A token is spent only when a job is actually claimed, and the refill is computed from elapsed time, so there's no background process topping up buckets.

Backlog quota at submit

-- Lock the tenant row so two concurrent submits can't both squeeze under the limit.
SELECT max_queued INTO lim FROM jobqueue.tenants WHERE tenant_id = p_tenant FOR UPDATE;
SELECT count(*) INTO queued FROM jobqueue.jobs WHERE tenant_id = p_tenant AND status = 'queued';
IF queued + p_count > lim THEN
  RAISE EXCEPTION 'tenant % over its queue quota (% queued, limit %)', p_tenant, queued, lim USING ERRCODE = 'P0429';
END IF;

The API maps SQLSTATE P0429 to HTTP 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header. Limiting how fast a tenant may call the submit API (requests per second) is a separate, HTTP-level limit; the same token-bucket row works for it, or use Redis if the API is very busy (see the Redis field guide).

Tested

postgres_queue_lab/tenant_limits_test.py runs the prototype with 20 workers competing. Tenant A is noisy (1,000 jobs); B–E have 50 each. Each job takes 20 ms. Every claim and finish is logged, and peaks are computed from the logs afterwards:

8. Changes to make before scaling

In order of value for effort. The first three are what turns the "as built" rows of the sizing table into "tuned".

1. Many jobs per worker process, one batched heartbeat

Run jobs as threads or asyncio tasks inside one process with a pool of 2 connections. Heartbeat all of the process's jobs in one statement, still fenced per job on (id, attempts):

UPDATE jobqueue.jobs j
SET locked_until = now() + interval '30 seconds', heartbeat_at = now(), heartbeats = heartbeats + 1, progress = h.progress
FROM unnest(%(ids)s::bigint[], %(attempts)s::int[], %(progress)s::int[]) AS h(id, attempt, progress)
WHERE j.id = h.id AND j.attempts = h.attempt AND j.locked_by = %(me)s AND j.status = 'running'
RETURNING j.id, j.cancel_requested;     -- ids missing from the result have lost their lease

2. Heartbeat every 10 seconds, lease 30 seconds

The 1s / 5s setting in the lab exists to make lease expiry visible in a demo. A lease of about 3 heartbeats tolerates one missed beat. Crash detection takes 30 seconds instead of 5, which is fine for background work, and heartbeat writes drop 10×.

3. Make heartbeats HOT

DROP INDEX jobqueue.jobs_lease;                          -- reclaim uses jobs_status_id (status, id) instead
ALTER TABLE jobqueue.jobs SET (fillfactor = 80);         -- room on each page for the new row version
VACUUM FULL jobqueue.jobs;                               -- rewrite existing pages (locks the table; do it in a quiet moment)

Measured: −36% WAL per busy worker-second and no index writes on heartbeats. Keep heartbeat-only columns out of all indexes.

4. Batch claims and acks

Claim LIMIT 10 per round trip when jobs are short, and ack a batch in one transaction. This roughly divides the per-job commit count by the batch size. Leave job_events inserts in the same transaction as the state change they describe.

5. Vacuum the hot table aggressively

ALTER TABLE jobqueue.jobs SET (autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor = 0.01, autovacuum_vacuum_threshold = 1000,
                               autovacuum_vacuum_cost_limit = 2000);
ALTER TABLE jobqueue.workers SET (fillfactor = 50);      -- tiny table, rewritten every second

6. Retention

Delete finished jobs in batches (for example every minute, LIMIT 5000, so no single transaction is huge), or move them to an archive table. Partition job_events by day and drop old partitions instead of deleting rows. Schedule it with pg_cron or a small periodic job in the app.

7. A cheaper UI

Count only queued and running live (they're small and indexed), show finished totals from a per-minute rollup or the reltuples estimate, list queues from a queues table, and refresh every 5 seconds. Then the UI's cost no longer grows with history.

8. Connections and NOTIFY

Put PgBouncer (transaction pooling) in front for the worker and API pools. LISTEN needs a real session, so keep one direct listener connection per process, or at high rates drop NOTIFY and poll every 1–2 seconds. Heartbeat transactions can use SET LOCAL synchronous_commit = off: a lost heartbeat after a crash is harmless, and those commits stop waiting on the WAL flush.

10. Sharing one Postgres with the application

A common starting point: the application already has one Postgres instance, so should the job queue live there too? Usually yes at first. The biggest benefit is something no broker gives you: creating a job in the same transaction as the business data. If the order row commits, its "send confirmation" job commits too. If it rolls back, so does the job. No outbox, no lost or orphaned jobs.

The cost is that the queue's writes compete with the application's. Here's what is shared, and what goes wrong:

Shared resourceHow the queue hurts the appHow the app hurts the queue
WAL, disk IOPS, checkpointsEvery claim, ack and heartbeat goes through the same WAL and fsync as app commits. A burst of 100,000 jobs is a burst of writes the app's commits wait behind.A big app migration or bulk load slows claims and acks.
Connections (max_connections)Workers can take connections the app needs, and the app gets "too many clients" errors. An app connection leak starves the workers.
Vacuum (autovacuum_max_workers, default 3, for the whole instance)The jobs table needs vacuuming constantly and keeps the vacuum workers busy, so the app's big tables wait longer. The classic failure: one long app transaction (a report, a stuck session left "idle in transaction") stops vacuum from removing dead job rows anywhere in that database. The claim index fills with dead entries and every claim slows down until it ends.
Replicas and backupsQueue churn is copied to every read replica and into the backups and point-in-time-recovery logs. Replica lag goes up for app reads.—
CPU and memorySmall for a tuned queue. The UI's full-table counts are the exception (52 ms each at 1M jobs).—

How much is safe to share?

Measure the queue's share of the instance instead of guessing. pg_stat_statements (Postgres 13+) records WAL bytes per query:

SELECT round(100.0 * sum(wal_bytes) FILTER (WHERE query ILIKE '%jobqueue.%') / sum(wal_bytes), 1) AS queue_wal_pct,
       round(100.0 * sum(calls)     FILTER (WHERE query ILIKE '%jobqueue.%') / sum(calls), 1)     AS queue_calls_pct
FROM pg_stat_statements;
Queue's share of WALApp latency (p95) when the queue is busyVerdict
Under ~10–20%No changeShare. Nothing to do.
~20–40%Small, visible rise during burstsApply the tuning and the guard rails below; plan the move.
Over ~40%, or the app's p95 tracks queue burstsClearly affectedMove the queue off the app's instance.

For scale, from the sizing table: the tuned design at 10 jobs/s writes ~25 KB/s of WAL and 30 commits/s, which is noise on almost any app database. At 100 jobs/s it's ~250 KB/s and ~300 commits/s, which is often a big share of a small app's write load.

Guard rails when sharing

-- Workers log in as their own role, so they can never take every connection.
CREATE ROLE queue_worker LOGIN PASSWORD '...' CONNECTION LIMIT 40;
GRANT USAGE ON SCHEMA jobqueue TO queue_worker;
GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON ALL TABLES IN SCHEMA jobqueue TO queue_worker;

-- Stop app sessions from holding back vacuum (the classic failure above).
ALTER ROLE app SET idle_in_transaction_session_timeout = '60s';
ALTER ROLE app SET statement_timeout = '30s';             -- reporting roles get their own, longer limit

-- Heartbeats don't need to wait for the disk flush: losing one in a crash is harmless.
-- In the worker's heartbeat transaction:
SET LOCAL synchronous_commit = off;

Plus the tuning steps (batched heartbeats every 10s, HOT-friendly indexes, aggressive autovacuum on jobs, retention) and an alert on the queue's WAL share.

The ladder: how to separate gradually

StepWhat it isolatesWhat you give up
1. Same database, own schema (this lab)Names and permissions onlyNothing. Enqueue is transactional with app data.
2. Separate database on the same instanceVacuum: a long app transaction no longer holds back vacuum on the jobs table (except through replication slots or standby feedback, which apply to the whole instance). Connection limits per database. Transactional enqueue (no cross-database transactions). Still shares WAL, disk, CPU, memory and backups.
3. Dedicated Postgres instance for the queueEverything: WAL, IOPS, connections, vacuum, replicas Transactional enqueue; you now need an outbox (app writes an outbox row in its own transaction, a relay copies it to the queue). One more database to run and pay for.
4. Broker for delivery, Postgres for job stateDelivery load (claims, polling, heartbeats, worker connections) leaves Postgres entirely Exact SQL-based limits and atomic claim + state change; you coordinate two systems (see section 13).

Step 2 is underrated: it costs nothing, fixes the vacuum problem, and is a one-line connection-string change, as long as no code enqueues jobs inside an app transaction. Step 3 is where the money starts (section 12).

11. When to move to a message broker

There's no single number. These are the signals, roughly in the order teams hit them. One strong signal or two moderate ones is a good reason to move delivery to a broker.

SignalThreshold (rule of thumb)Why Postgres struggles
Sustained throughputOver ~500 jobs/s as built; over ~1,000–2,000 jobs/s even tuned. The laptop ceiling was ~1,550/s with zero-work jobs. Each job is ~3 commits and ~2.5 KB of WAL, all fsynced. A broker appends to a log and acks in batches.
Hurting the app databaseQueue over ~40% of the shared instance's WAL, or app p95 latency rises with queue bursts, and a dedicated instance isn't wanted Shared WAL, IOPS, vacuum and connections (section 10).
BurstsMillions of jobs submitted within minutes, a few times a day A burst is a write storm on the database: index growth, then a big vacuum. A broker absorbs bursts as cheap appends.
Pickup latencyJobs must start within ~10 ms at hundreds per second or more Polling adds up to one interval of delay. NOTIFY is instant but serialises commits under load.
Very short jobsJobs that take milliseconds, at high volume The bookkeeping (claim, ack, history) costs more than the work. A broker's per-message overhead is far smaller.
Worker countMore than ~500 worker processes even after pooling Postgres connections are processes. Brokers handle tens of thousands of consumer connections easily.
Vacuum can't keep upn_dead_tup on jobs keeps growing after tuning; claim latency creeps up through the day Every job leaves 3+ dead row versions. A log-based broker deletes whole segments instead.
Many consumers per eventSeveral services must react to the same event, or you need replay That's a log (Kafka) or pub/sub, not a work queue. See messaging tools compared.

Reasons to stay on Postgres, even at higher volume

  • Long-running jobs (minutes to hours). Brokers prefer short ones: RabbitMQ closes channels that hold a message unacked past consumer_timeout (30 minutes by default), and SQS visibility timeouts can't exceed 12 hours. A Postgres lease plus heartbeat has no ceiling.
  • Jobs must commit with business data. Anything else needs an outbox.
  • Exact per-tenant limits and fairness (section 7). Brokers give you per-queue limits, not "max 3 running for tenant A" across a shared queue.
  • You query jobs: "all failed jobs for tenant X yesterday", "cancel everything for this customer". A broker can't answer those.
  • A small team. One fewer stateful system to run, secure, upgrade and be on call for.

SQS ranges: batched (10 per call) to unbatched. Self-hosting RabbitMQ on an existing Kubernetes cluster replaces the $600 with node capacity, but not with zero: you take on its upgrades, storage and on-call.

What the table says

  • Below ~100 jobs/s, the cheapest option is the one you already have. Sharing the app's Postgres costs almost nothing, and a broker's real cost is mostly people: another system to deploy, secure, monitor, upgrade and debug, plus an outbox to keep enqueue reliable. For a small team, that easily costs more than any of the infrastructure lines.
  • SQS is the cheapest broker by far at low and medium volume, because it's per request with no minimum. A RabbitMQ cluster has a ~$600/month floor even when idle.
  • A broker doesn't remove the database cost if you keep job state (status, history, results) in Postgres, and job management needs it (section 13). What it removes is the delivery load. After tuning, the delivery load is the smaller part: at 100 jobs/s, about 96% of the WAL is job state (2.5 KB per job) and only ~4% is heartbeats.
  • Around 1,000 jobs/s the costs cross. A dedicated Postgres sized for the full queue load (~$2,500/month) costs about the same as SQS plus a smaller state database, and the broker handles bursts and latency better. That, not cost, is usually the deciding factor.

13. Where brokers keep their data, and why you still need a database

Brokers don't use Postgres or MySQL underneath. Each has its own storage engine, built for messages that are written once, read once and deleted:

The pattern: brokers store messages in flight; they are not job databases. Once a message is acked it's gone (Kafka keeps it, but you can't query it by tenant or status). A job management system needs to answer "what's the status of job 123", "show this tenant's failed jobs", "how many jobs did this customer run this month", and offer cancel and retry. Those need a database. So real systems with a broker almost always look like this:

submit:  INSERT job (status = queued) in Postgres  ──►  publish {job_id} to the broker     (via an outbox)
worker:  receive {job_id}  ──►  UPDATE status = running  ──►  do the work  ──►  UPDATE status = succeeded  ──►  ack the broker
crash:   no ack before the timeout  ──►  the broker redelivers to another worker  (replaces lease + heartbeat + reclaim)
UI:      reads job state from Postgres, never from the broker

What moves to the broker: claiming, polling, lease expiry and redelivery, most worker connections, burst absorption. What stays in Postgres: job state and history, which are most of the WAL once heartbeats are tuned. That's why Celery has a "result backend" (Redis or a database) next to its broker, and why Temporal and Hatchet are built on a database outright. Moving to a broker changes how jobs are delivered; it doesn't remove the need to store them.

Summary

Start with the queue in the app's Postgres, in its own schema, with the guard rails from section 10. Move it to a separate database, then a dedicated instance, as its share of the load grows. Add a broker for delivery when you hit the signals in section 11, usually somewhere between a few hundred and ~2,000 jobs/s, or earlier for bursts, latency or fan-out, and keep job state in Postgres either way.
